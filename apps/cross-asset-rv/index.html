How the scanner works

Three kinds of relationship

Arbitrage relationships have a mechanism that a trader can use to force prices together: ETF creation and redemption, cash-and-carry for index futures, depositary conversion of ADRs, cross-border settlement of interlisted shares, Berkshire's class A to class B conversion. Economic links (share classes without conversion, dual-listed companies, ADRs with conversion limits such as Taiwan's, calendar and crack spreads, same-index funds) have a reason to converge but nothing that forces it. Statistical relationships (sector and stock pairs, index pairs, curve and cross-market bond spreads, carry) are co-movements; the scanner never calls them arbitrage, however good their test statistics look.

From a deviation to an executable opportunity

A signal starts when the spread's z-score passes the entry level. It is only called executable when every check passes:

  • the legs' closing prices are within an hour of each other (Tokyo, Taipei and Hong Kong closes are 13–16 hours before New York, so their apparent ADR "premiums" are partly the market move in between);
  • for arbitrage relationships, the deviation lies outside the no-arbitrage band (conversion fees, stamp duties and transaction taxes, half-spreads, FX conversion);
  • the expected convergence over the next half-lives covers round-trip costs after carry, funding and borrow;
  • the short leg can be borrowed (Indian ordinary shares cannot be shorted by foreign investors, for example);
  • the spread shows mean reversion in its recent history (Dickey-Fuller test, not a normal t-test) and its half-life is longer than a day;
  • the data are current.

Timing and costs

Backtests trade at the close after the signal (next-close execution), pay stress-scaled half-spreads, commissions, transaction taxes and FX conversion on every leg, plus funding, borrow and hedge-rebalancing costs. The research tab shows why the next-close rule matters: with same-close fills, … relationships look like Sharpe-above-1 arbitrages; with next-close fills, … do.

Models

Engle-Granger and Johansen cointegration tests (MacKinnon p-values), augmented Dickey-Fuller, Kalman-filter and rolling hedge ratios, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck half-lives, a two-state Markov-switching model of the spread, and a Bayesian comparison of mean reversion against a random walk with posterior-predictive forecasts. Relationship stability is tracked with rolling tests and CUSUM. Multiple testing is controlled with Benjamini-Hochberg, Bonferroni, deflated Sharpe ratios and White's reality check.

Data policy

Rates, FX fixings, sovereign yields, money-market rates and EIA energy prices come from public-domain or open sources (US Treasury, Federal Reserve, New York Fed, ECB, Bundesbank, Japan's Ministry of Finance, Bank of Canada, Reserve Bank of Australia, Bank of England, EIA, OECD); those series are fully interactive in Build a pair. Relationships that use vendor prices (ETFs, ADRs and home listings, index futures, issuer NAVs, Moody's and ICE yields) are shown as derived analytics only: the spread, the hedged position's P&L, costs and backtests, never the instruments' own prices.

Nothing here is investment advice. Results describe historical data under stated assumptions.
