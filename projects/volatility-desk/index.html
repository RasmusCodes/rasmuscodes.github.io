A cross-asset volatility desk on thirty years of option data

Five surface models compared on 371 dates, seventeen option strategies backtested day by day, walk-forward forecasts and dispersion trades, with a working volatility terminal below.

A research platform for equity-index volatility, built on OptionMetrics and Cboe option data through WRDS: every S&P 500 option quote from 1996 to November 2026, chains for SPY, QQQ and IWM, standardized surfaces for the fifty largest index members, and VIX futures. It fits volatility surfaces, backtests option structures with Greek P&L attribution, forecasts volatility and correlation out of sample, and tests whether the forecasts make money after costs.

The terminal below runs entirely in your browser. Pick an underlying and a date (calm days and crisis days from 2000 to 2026), turn the implied-volatility surface in 3D and compare the five models, build an option strategy, then move spot, volatility and time and see where the P&L comes from. The Scenarios tab replays fifteen historical stress episodes on your position; Backtests shows thirty years of results for seventeen strategies.

What I found #

The closest fit is not the safest surface. On 371 S&P 500 month-ends, SVI misses the market by a median 0.24 volatility points and puts 89% of quotes inside the bid-ask, but its extrapolated wings carry calendar arbitrage. Extended SSVI is free of static arbitrage on every date at twice the error; Heston fits worst at 0.97 points.

Selling index volatility paid before costs; costs decide most of the rest. A short at-the-money straddle rolled monthly earned 5.3% of notional a year before costs and 4.0% after, paid for with crash losses (−10.5% of notional on the worst day). Delta-hedged every day, the same straddle earned 3.5% a year before costs, 1.8% after realistic costs, and nothing after stressed costs. Calendars, diagonals, butterflies and risk reversals made almost nothing before costs and lost money after. The delta-hedged short 25-delta put and call had the best risk-adjusted results.

Forecasts are good; timing with them is not. A log-HAR model with the VIX forecasts S&P 500 realized variance 7.5% and 26% better than an exponentially weighted average at one and five days, and implied plus realized correlation predicts next month’s correlation best (out-of-sample R² 36.5%). But no forecast-driven rule for timing the variance premium beat a constant allocation after costs; volatility targeting cut the index’s drawdown from −84% to −65% and nudged its Sharpe ratio up.

There is a correlation premium. Implied correlation between the fifty largest S&P 500 members exceeded next month’s realized correlation by 0.04 on average (t = 5.1), with severe losses when correlations spike.

Data and its limits #

Option data are licensed (OptionMetrics, Cboe, Datastream and CRSP through WRDS) and are never published: the terminal ships fitted model parameters, aggregate statistics and backtest results only, so it draws the models’ surfaces and the quoted range rather than the quotes themselves. The two vendors’ quotes differ on stressed days; the history switches from OptionMetrics to Cboe in September 2025. Single stocks have standardized surfaces but no chains here, so dispersion trades are variance-based. Backtests are historical simulations with estimated costs, not evidence of a tradable edge.

How it’s built #

A Python package (pricing, data, surfaces, strategies, backtest, forecasting, dispersion) with 35 tests that run in CI on synthetic option chains, five research reports, and a vanilla-JavaScript terminal that reprices everything in the browser, including Heston by the COS method, and checks itself against values computed in Python.
