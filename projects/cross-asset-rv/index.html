A cross-asset relative-value scanner: which dislocations are real?

191 pricing relationships across ETFs, futures, ADRs, FX, sovereign and corporate bonds, commodities and funding markets, each checked for a convergence mechanism, synchronous prices, costs, borrow and multiple testing before anything is called executable.

Two prices that should move together sometimes don’t. The question that matters is whether a trader could have done anything about it. This scanner searches 191 relationships for dislocations and labels each one by what links the prices:

  • Arbitrage: a mechanism lets a trader force convergence, such as ETF creation and redemption, cash-and-carry for index futures, depositary conversion of ADRs, cross-border settlement of interlisted shares, or Berkshire’s class A to class B conversion.
  • Economic link: there is a reason to converge, but nothing forces it. Examples are share classes without conversion, dual-listed companies, ADRs with conversion limits, calendar and crack spreads.
  • Statistical: the prices have co-moved, nothing more. Sector and stock pairs, curve and cross-market bond spreads and carry trades fall here, and the scanner never calls them arbitrage.

A signal is called executable only if the legs’ closing prices are synchronous, the deviation clears the no-arbitrage band (fees, stamp duties, transaction taxes, half-spreads, FX), the expected convergence pays for costs, carry, funding and borrow, the short leg can be borrowed, and the spread actually mean-reverts.

Try it below. Filter by asset class or relationship strength, move the entry threshold, and open any relationship to see its spread, hedge ratio, past convergence, costs and risk, or run a hypothetical trade. Build a pair runs the cointegration tests and a backtest in your browser on open government and central-bank data.

What I found #

Data runs to 20 November 2026. The same rule, fixed in advance, applies to every relationship: enter at a z-score of 2, exit at 0.5, stop at 4, trade at the next close, conservative costs.

  • Same-close fills invent arbitrage. If you assume a trade fills at the very close that produced its signal, 73 relationships earn a net Sharpe ratio above 1; arbitrage relationships have a median of 2.2. Fill at the next close, which a trader can actually do, and none is above 1, with a median of -0.40. The apparent profits come from bid-ask bounce at the close, stale fund NAVs and prices taken hours apart. An Asian ADR’s premium predicts the next day’s home-market return (median correlation 0.55), and an international ETF’s “premium” predicts the next NAV change.
  • Multiple testing removes what is left. 10 of 181 strategies are positive at the 5% level, about the 9 expected by chance. None survives a Benjamini-Hochberg or Bonferroni correction, and White’s reality check gives the best one a p-value of 0.27.
  • The cointegration screen finds what chance predicts. Over the last three years, 52 of 1,221 pairs (4.3%) pass an Engle-Granger test at 5%, while independent random walks pass 5.5% of the time, and one pair survives the correction. Selected pairs did stay cointegrated more often than the base rate (21% against 5%), but traded out of sample they lost money after costs.
  • Carry is the one consistent earner, and it is a risk premium, not an arbitrage: 12 of 13 FX carry backtests are positive, with a median Sharpe of 0.29, but none is significant on its own.
  • Kalman, rolling and 1:1 hedge ratios perform alike once costs are paid; a hedge frozen at formation decays. Gating trades on a regime-switching filter or a Bayesian test of mean reversion changes the median Sharpe by less than 0.02.

The report keeps the failures: an FX “triangle” built from two official fixings taken six hours apart, WTI’s April 2020 contango blowing through the full-carry bound because storage ran out, Berkshire’s conversion bound “violated” by closing-print noise, TSMC’s ADR premium that never converges because Taiwan restricts new ADRs, and the corporate actions the price vendor adjusted on one listing but not the other.

How it works #

  • Data: 381 instruments from 12 sources. Rates, FX fixings, sovereign yields, money markets and energy come from the US Treasury, Federal Reserve, New York Fed, ECB, Bundesbank, Japan’s Ministry of Finance, Bank of Canada, Reserve Bank of Australia, Bank of England, EIA and OECD. Equities, ETFs, ADRs, futures and indices come from a price vendor, and ETF NAVs from the issuer. Every download is cached with its source, licence and checksum.
  • Models: Engle-Granger and Johansen tests with MacKinnon p-values (matched to statsmodels), Kalman-filter hedge ratios, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck half-lives judged with Dickey-Fuller rather than normal critical values, a two-state Markov-switching model, a Bayesian comparison of mean reversion against a random walk, change-point detection and rolling stability tests. The Bayesian part exists because the usual flat-prior posterior reports about 99% “mean reversion” for pure random walks.
  • Costs and risk: stress-scaled half-spreads, commissions, square-root market impact, stamp duties and transaction taxes in London, Hong Kong, Taipei, Mumbai, Seoul, Zurich and Paris, FX conversion, ADR conversion fees, funding and borrow, and a full re-trade at every futures roll. Each trade reports its maximum adverse excursion, and each relationship its basis risk, currency exposure, liquidity and capacity.
  • Validation: in-sample to 2015 and out-of-sample since, Benjamini-Hochberg, Bonferroni and Holm corrections, deflated Sharpe ratios, White’s reality check, random-walk null rates and rolling formation-to-test persistence. 41 tests cover the models, the engine and the scanners. The browser engine reproduces the Python backtests.

Limits #

  • Daily closing prices only. Synchronised intraday quotes would settle which timing-limited dislocations were real; they aren’t freely available.
  • Corporate bonds are covered through indices and ETFs, not single bonds, and without FX forwards covered interest parity can’t be measured.
  • Borrow availability and fees are assumptions by market tier, not locate data.
  • Vendor prices appear on this page only as derived analytics: spreads, hedged-position P&L and backtest results, never the instruments’ own prices.
