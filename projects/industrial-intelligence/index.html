Reading industrial demand before the earnings release

Factory orders, power-plant pipelines, grid load, customers’ capital spending, permits and tenders, mapped to 17 industrial companies and tested point in time. They nowcast revenue slightly better than company history, lead nothing, and predict no excess returns.

Industrial Intelligence is a platform that tracks physical economic activity (factory orders, planned power plants, electricity demand, the capital spending of the companies’ customers, construction registrations, utility tenders) and asks whether it tells you an industrial company’s revenue and margins before the company reports them. It covers 17 US-listed equipment makers and contractors, among them Caterpillar, Vertiv, Eaton, GE Vernova, Quanta and the three big chip-tool makers, and maps ABB, Siemens Energy and Schneider Electric without modelling them.

The interactive dashboard lets you pick a company and inspect its segments, its mapped indicators against reported revenue, a forecast you can change, its historical forecast errors and a dated timeline of contracts, projects and announcements.

How it works #

  • Sources. Eighteen sources were probed and ranked by availability, reliability and economic relevance; twelve public ones are used: SEC filings (financials, 879 XBRL documents with segment data, 1,055 press releases), Census factory orders and construction spending with every published vintage, EIA’s monthly power-plant inventory since 2015, hourly grid demand, three grid operators’ interconnection queues, Texas data center registrations, EU utility tenders, job postings and trade flows. Where a site’s robots.txt or terms said no (the BLS API, ERCOT), or a source sits behind a login, it is listed as not used.
  • Point in time. Every value carries the moment it became public. Revisions are new rows, not overwrites. Forecasts are made one second before each earnings release, so a model only ever sees what was published by then.
  • A mapping layer written first. Each company is linked to the indicators that should drive it, with the segment, the expected timing and the channel, before any model saw the data. Its profile (segments, geography, customers, backlog, pricing, margin drivers) quotes its own 10-K, and all 250 quotes are checked against the filing text by the test suite.
  • Models and tests. Baselines (random walk, the company’s own history), a composite of the mapped indicators, a partially pooled Bayesian version, ridge, gradient boosting and backlog models, refit every quarter. 2016–2020 is the development period and 2021–2026 the holdout. Diebold-Mariano and Clark-West tests treat each quarter as one observation.

What it found #

  • A small, real nowcast gain. Just before the release, the indicator model misses reported revenue growth by 7.39 percentage points on average in the holdout, against 7.50 for the company’s own history and 7.82 for a random walk. The indicators carry information history lacks (Clark-West p = 0.002), but the accuracy gain is too small to be significant on its own (Diebold-Mariano p = 0.23), and there was none in 2016–2020.
  • Where it works: data center suppliers, grid equipment, chip-tool makers (2–4% better in the holdout). Not for Caterpillar, Cummins and United Rentals (3% worse).
  • Coincident, not leading. One or two quarters before a release the indicators add nothing. Their information arrives with the quarter itself.
  • A careless backtest would have said more. On today’s revised data with no publication lags, the holdout gain reads 2.6% instead of 1.5%.
  • More model is worse. Gradient boosting was 14% worse than company history and a ridge on every indicator 24% worse: 650 company-quarters is not enough data for them.
  • No excess returns. The indicators’ extra information predicted neither earnings-day returns nor the drift into the release (CRSP data through 2024, 12 companies). Predicting revenue and predicting the stock are different questions, and only the first got a positive answer.

Four case studies #

  • Data center power and cooling. Hyperscaler capex appears to lead Vertiv’s revenue by two quarters in sample, but out of sample the lead disappears; HVAC orders and cloud capex help the nowcast slightly.
  • Caterpillar equipment demand. The composite fails, mostly because sales follow dealer inventories. One series, US construction-machinery orders, leads by about a quarter at every horizon, but does not survive a multiple-testing correction: a hypothesis for the next earnings seasons.
  • Electrical equipment and grid. Utility capex is too smooth to be a quarterly signal, and price indexes do not beat simple mean reversion for margins.
  • Order backlogs and conversion. Backlogs grew faster than revenue at all ten companies that report them, so every backlog-to-revenue rule fails; the indicators do track implied order intake.

Limits #

  • No consensus estimates are licensed, so “surprise” means surprise against a statistical baseline, not against the market.
  • Some series exist only as revised history (Census data center construction, grid load); for them the backtest assumes a publication lag and cannot replay revisions.
  • UN Comtrade’s anonymous quota cut the trade series short, satellite data need accounts the project does not have, and companies outside the SEC’s quarterly XBRL (ABB, Siemens Energy, Schneider) are mapped but not modelled.
  • 17 companies and about 40 quarters each is a small sample: company-level results are noisy.

How it’s built #

A Python package with one polite HTTP client for every source (robots.txt, throttling, a raw cache with checksums), a point-in-time store, an XBRL replay that derives each quarter in the order filings were accepted, and a walk-forward engine with a leakage audit. 41 tests cover the replay, the as-of feature engine, the statistics and the parsers, and recompute sampled features from raw observations to check the point-in-time logic. The dashboard is static: everything it shows is exported as JSON, so it runs without the repository.
