From −$29 to +$99 a round: teaching a bot to trade Figgie

How a perfect card counter learned to read the market, found a habit in its opponents to exploit, and ended up running on its own. Fifteen versions, 142 live rounds, and the ideas that didn’t work.

The first version of my Figgie bot knew the exact probability of every possible deck at every moment of the game. It lost $29 a round.

Its opponents, the bots on figgie.com, were happy to sell it the suits they knew were worthless and to buy its cheap offers on the one suit that paid. In one round it finished holding ten clubs. The goal suit was diamonds.

Fifteen versions and 142 live rounds later, the same opponents lose to it. Over its last 41 rounds the bot made $81 a round on average, and the newest version opens its own tables, plays, and moves to a fresh table when its opponents run low on chips. This is how it got there, including the ideas that failed.

Figgie in two minutes #

Figgie is a card game Jane Street designed to feel like trading in an open-outcry pit. Four players each put $50 in the pot and are dealt ten cards from a 40-card deck. The deck is lopsided: one suit has 12 cards, two have 10 and one has 8, and nobody is told which is which. The suit of the same colour as the 12-card suit is the goal suit.

For four minutes, anyone can bid for or offer any suit, and anyone can take a bid or an offer. At the bell, each goal-suit card pays $10 from the pot, and whoever holds the most goal cards takes the rest: $100, or $120 when the goal suit has eight cards.

Figure 2. An example deal. Spades has 12 cards, so the goal is clubs, the other black suit.

So the game is about information. Your hand hints at which suit has 12 cards. Everyone else’s trading hints at what their hands told them. A good player counts cards, reads the market, and trades on the answer before the market has finished giving it away.

There are only 12 possible decks (four choices for the 12-card suit, then three for the 8-card suit), so a program can track the exact probability of each one. That’s where I started.

The setup #

The bot plays on figgie.com, where Figgie runs in the browser, against the site’s own bots set to Hard. I set myself three rules:

  • Bots only. Private tables where every other seat is one of figgie.com’s bots. Never against people, and only for play money.
  • Only what a player sees. Its own hand, the bids and offers, the trades and the clock. Nothing about the other players’ hands, even though the web page holds more.
  • Human speed. At most four orders a second, sent through the same functions the site’s buttons call.

The strategy lives in about 4,400 lines of Python: a rules-accurate game engine, a simulator with fifteen kinds of opponents, and analysis tools. A 2,900-line userscript carries a line-for-line JavaScript port of the strategy into the page, plus an adapter that finds the game’s state inside figgie.com’s app. A test harness checks that the two versions compute the same probabilities and send the same orders.

Figure 3. What the bot does with what it sees, many times a second.

Version 1: perfect counting, naive trading #

v1 did the maths right. It updated its probabilities for all 12 decks on every trade, valued each card from them, and made markets around those values, bidding a little below and offering a little above.

That’s how you lose money to people who know more than you do. When v1’s bid was hit, it was usually because the seller knew the suit was dead. When its offer was lifted, the buyer usually knew it was the goal. Traders call this adverse selection: the trades you get are the ones the other side wanted. v1 ignored what everyone else’s trading said, so its fills were systematically bad, and it often didn’t know the goal even at the bell: in five of nine rounds it put 30% or less on it. It lost $261 over nine rounds.

Listening to the market #

One recorded round showed the way out. Every bid of $10 or more in that round was for the goal suit, while the dead suits changed hands at $1 to $4. The other bots knew, and their bids said so.

v2 treats the highest price anyone bids or pays for a suit as a vote that it’s the goal, and a low offer as a vote against. It also stopped being eager. Its bids start low and climb a dollar every three seconds, and it takes someone else’s quote only with a 20% edge. v2 made $25 a round.

v3 fixed the next leak. Early in a round every suit is still a plausible goal, so each card looked cheap and the bot sold into early bids, often to the bots that suspected that suit was the goal. But holding a card is an option. Once the market reveals the goal, a goal card pays $10 and sells to players chasing the bonus for $11 to $19, and a dead card can still be sold for a couple of dollars. v3 never sells a card for less than that option is worth, and the premium fades to nothing by the bell. It was the first version with a statistically clear profit: $42 a round, with eight of ten rounds in profit.

Then I let the data decide. By v6 the logs held 42 live rounds with the dealt hands, every suit’s top bid and the true goal, enough to fit the signal instead of guessing it. figgie.com’s bots bid $10 to $15 for dead suits all the time, so only bids above about $17 really single out the goal. v7 scores bids that way. It also counts a bid only once it has stood on the book in two different seconds, so a bid flashed and cancelled can’t move it.

v8 found the strongest signal. Every Hard bot bids the goal suit up at some point in a round, while a dead suit usually draws a big bid from only one of them. So the lowest of the three bots’ top bids for each suit is a near-perfect tell: in ten rounds it was $7 to $10 for the goal and $3 to $6 for every dead suit. One bluffer can’t fake that.

Figure 4. The bot’s probability for each suit in one live round. The lead changes hands for two and a half minutes before the market settles on diamonds.

Figure 5. From v8 on, the bot was at least 90% sure of the right suit by the bell in most rounds.

Then came the surprise. v8 finished every round with the right suit as its favourite, at 97% on average against v7’s 74%, and its profit didn’t move: $33.50 a round, inside the noise of every version since v3. Knowing the answer at the bell is worth very little. The money is made in the first two or three minutes, while the answer is still uncertain. v9 attacked that window from the other side: early in a round, whoever buys from the bot probably knows more than it does, so its offers are priced as if the buyer does.

Good ideas that lost money #

Two failures taught me more than most of the successes.

v5 was about the bonus. With a 10-card goal suit, five cards can only tie for the majority, and six win it. The bot kept stopping at five, and figgie.com’s bots kept collecting the strays: in seven rounds of v3 and v4 the bonus was split 5-5, leaving $350 on the table. So v5 valued the bonus as if a rival would end up with every goal card the bot lacked, which made the sixth card worth chasing. It got there twice. But the same assumption made small holdings look worthless, so in six rounds it sold the goal cards it had been dealt, cheaply. It made $16 a round, its worst result since v1. v6 kept the idea only for when the bot already holds half the goal suit, where the next card decides between a tie and a win, and took the whole bonus in five of ten rounds.

The second failure was a better model. I built one that learned each opponent’s tells (their top bids, their cheap offers, what they accumulated) and corrected the bot’s probabilities with them, refitting at every bell. As a forecaster it worked: against opponents with tells it put more probability on the true goal. And it lost $2 a round in simulation. Small shifts in belief made the bot commit to the right suit a little less often, and the bonus goes to whoever commits. At one table it won the bonus in 68% of rounds instead of 72%, and that was its whole loss there. A better forecast doesn’t automatically make a better decision.

An exploit hiding in the logs #

From v9 on, the bot logged every quote and trade. Reading the logs turned up a habit of figgie.com’s bots. When a player offered or sold a card for $1 or $2, another player usually bid the same suit back up to $4 or more, typically 11 to 12 seconds later: after 96% of 166 such cards in one batch of logs, and 76% of 135 in the next. None of those cheap cards was the goal. The bots dump dead cards and then want them back.

v10 buys those cards and resells them. Being wrong about a pattern like that is the real risk, so most of the code is about stopping. The bot holds only a few unsold cards at a time, counts its own resales double when it judges the pattern, and shuts the exploit off for the round after two net failures or $6 lost. In the simulator I wrote a ring of bots that fakes the pattern to bait it, and the caps hold the damage to about $6.50 a game.

Live, it worked from the first round. Resale made $73 over v10’s ten rounds, $153 over v11’s, $203 over v12’s and $163 over v13’s, across more than 300 resales, none of them sold at a loss.

Then I wanted the bot to find the pattern itself. v12 replaces the hand-written rule with a learner. Every offer another player shows at $5 or less is a question the tape answers within seconds: had the bot bought that card, would someone have bid $2 more for the suit within 45 seconds? So every offer at the table is a labelled example, not only the bot’s own trades. An online Bayesian logistic regression learns the answer from generic features such as the price, the time left, recent bids and the seller’s recent offers. Starting from nothing, it rediscovered “$2 or less resells” within its first live round, and that became its strongest feature. At figgie.com that only matches the rule I’d found by hand. The point is the next table, where the rule is wrong: the learner adjusts and the rule doesn’t.

Opponents that fight back #

Up to v12 the simulator’s opponents were mostly benchmarks the bot was built to beat. For v13 I wrote opponents built to beat it: a spoofer that flashes big bids on dead suits, rings that fake the resale pattern, a “pump” ring that trades a dead suit among itself at $18 so the tape shows everyone paying up, a sniper, a front-runner, a buyer that never shows a bid, and one that learns what the bot will pay. A test bench, the arena, plays every change in paired sessions at 21 tables, with separate seeds for tuning, choosing and reporting. It scores a change by half its average gain and half its gain over the worst quarter of tables, so a change has to help broadly.

The pump ring took $63 a round: the bot was 98% sure of the decoy after 30 seconds. So v13 learns at every bell, from the revealed goal, how far to trust each of its market readings at this table, and a signal that lies there fades within a round or two. That fixed the belief but not the money. Three colluders who know the goal still take about $60 a round, because they sell the bot dead cards and buy its goal cards whatever it believes. With its market reading switched off entirely, it loses about as much. Some opponents can’t be beaten, and it helps to know which.

The arena also said no a lot. An endgame routine, putting quotes back after the book clears, random quote timing and a stricter smart-money rule were all tried and dropped. A seeded search over 18 quoting and holding parameters found nothing that beat the hand-tuned values robustly on held-out tables.

Running it live is half the work #

figgie.com is a React Native web app that talks to its server over a socket. There’s no API for bots, so the userscript finds the game’s state by walking React’s internal component tree, listens to the same socket messages the page does, and sends orders through the same functions the buttons call. Keeping that running took as much work as the strategy:

  • Hidden tabs. Browsers starve background tabs. Timers stalled for up to a minute, stale quotes sat on the book, and figgie.com dropped the connection. The trading clock now runs in a Web Worker, and the script supplies the animation frames a hidden page doesn’t get.
  • Crashes. Moving to a new table crashed the page now and then. figgie.com’s game screen crashes if it renders with an empty player list, which can happen while it animates out. The script now sends the screen home, waits for it to unmount, and only then closes the old table.
  • Messages without an address. The site sends an empty “match closed” message that doesn’t say which table it means, and shows some notices with a browser alert that freezes the page until someone clicks OK. The script holds a “match closed” for three seconds and drops it if the round’s clock keeps ticking, logs the alerts instead of freezing on them, and leaves any round whose clock stands still.
  • Speed. The bot used to look for trades every 250 milliseconds, so a $1 offer could wait almost half a second, and figgie.com’s bots, which run on the server, often got there first. It now reacts to each new quote as soon as the page receives it, and most of its takes go out within a few milliseconds.

Then it learned to run without me. It opens a private table with three Hard bots, starts each round eight seconds after the last bell, and moves to a fresh table once any bot ends a round under $100. Chips carry over at a table and a broke bot can’t bid, so a long session at one table dries up the market; that is exactly what spoiled v13’s last two rounds. In its first long session the bot played 11 rounds at three tables it opened itself, with one page reload when figgie.com logged the guest out, and made $810.

Results #

Figure 7. Cumulative profit over every live round.

Across 142 live rounds the bot made $6,958 in play money. Every live test since v3 has been profitable. Over the last 41 rounds (v11 to v13.3) it averaged $81 a round, ±$18 at 95% confidence, with 34 of 41 rounds in profit.

Two caveats matter. First, ten rounds is a small sample: each version’s average carries a 95% interval of about ±$32 to ±$45, so most single steps in Figure 1 are noise. v13’s +$98.90, the best result, came from a version that plays almost exactly like v12 at figgie.com, by design; the extra $19 a round was the deals and the table. The trend across versions is real, and any one step mostly isn’t. That’s why most decisions were made on paired simulations (the same deals for both versions), replays of logged rounds and paper trading on logged order books, with live tests as the final check. Second, these are figgie.com’s bots, not people. They’re predictable in ways people aren’t, and the bot has known weaknesses, which the repository lists.

What I learned #

  1. Adverse selection comes first. Perfect arithmetic on your own information loses to people trading on theirs. Before asking what a card is worth, ask why someone is willing to trade it with you.
  2. Prices are information. The biggest gains came from reading other players’ bids, and the cleanest signal came from looking at the bids player by player instead of pooled.
  3. Optimise the decision, not the forecast. v8 knew the goal far more often and made no more money, and a better reader of opponents lost money. Measure what you’re paid for.
  4. Know your noise. Ten live rounds can’t tell a $10 improvement from luck. Paired simulations, replays of logged rounds and separate tuning, validation and holdout seeds did the deciding; live tests confirmed.
  5. Write the adversary. Opponents built to exploit the bot found holes no benchmark showed, and gave an honest answer about which ones it can’t close.
  6. The plumbing is half the work. Hidden tabs, page crashes and a message that doesn’t say which table it means have nothing to do with strategy, and every one of them cost money.

Try it #

The code, every live round and the analysis tools are on GitHub at github.com/RasmusCodes/figgie-bot, under the MIT licence. If you run the userscript, keep it to private tables against figgie.com’s bots, and check the site’s terms first.
