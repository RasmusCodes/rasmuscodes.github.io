1. Bottom line #

  • Recovery is derived, not assumed. Building the waterfall from the filings gives 34% recovery on the unsecured notes for defaults before January 2029. Depending on how much weight the AI-bust scenario gets, the range is 24-40%. S&P’s estimate is 25% and Fitch’s band is 31-50% (section 4).
  • The debt is cheap against the equity in one specific, tradeable way. At that recovery, the 9.75% 2031 notes imply 31% default by January 2029, and the shorter 2030 notes 29%. Deep out-of-the-money January 2029 puts imply 19-26%. Default cover through $10 puts costs about 74% of what the notes pay you to carry the same risk, and through $5 puts about 62%. The gap closes only if recovery is below about 10%.
  • CDS agrees with the bonds. Markit’s 5-year spread closed at 1,141bp on 22 Oct. At the same 34% recovery that implies 32% default by January 2029. CDS trades 29-66bp wider than the 2031-2032 notes.
  • It is not cheap in the classic structural sense. At the 85% long-dated implied vol, the CreditGrades model prices the notes about 3 points rich at 34% recovery. In October the stock fell 37% while the notes fell about 5 points, so equity has already cheapened against credit.
  • So skip the textbook “long bond, short stock at the model delta.” It loses $238k per $1mm face on a default tomorrow at 34% recovery, and $102k if the stock triples over the next year.
  • Run the tail version instead: long the 9.75% 2031 notes, plus January 2029 $10 and $20 puts sized so a default nets to zero at the low-end 24% recovery, plus a short of half the model stock delta.
  • Model result per $1mm face: about +$66k over 12 months if nothing moves (7.0% on capital) and +$162k if the notes converge to the equity-tail fair value.
    • Positive at every 12-month stock price from $8 to $120, and roughly flat (−$7k) if the stock triples to $160.
    • +$149k if CoreWeave defaults in six months at 34% recovery.
  • The converts are not rich, so there is no short-convert leg. At the derived recovery they screen 2-8 points cheap to the straights.
  • The risks that remain are real:
    • recovery below 24%;
    • credit-only selloffs (new secured debt, liability-management deals, downgrades);
    • an upward-sloping CDS curve, which LSEG cannot show you;
    • access: the notes were sold only to qualified institutional buyers (Rule 144A) or non-US persons (Regulation S), and the long-dated puts are thin.

4. Recovery on the notes: derived, not assumed #

Every comparison between the notes and the puts depends on what the notes recover in a default. This section builds that number from the filings, and every input has a named source.

Method. It follows the rating agencies’ recovery approach, with each input traced:

  1. Value at default. The present value of the cash EBITDA still to come from the contracted backlog after the default date. Add what the compute equipment is worth when the backlog ends, plus receivables and restricted cash. Unrestricted cash is assumed spent before default.
  2. Claims by priority. Each SPV’s term lenders take that SPV’s collateral first, and a shortfall on a recourse loan becomes a claim alongside the notes. The revolver and equipment financing come next. Last is the unsecured pool: notes, converts, six months’ interest, trade payables, lease-rejection claims and any term-loan shortfall.
  3. Waterfall. Run at each default date from now to the January 2029 put expiry and averaged, for three default scenarios.

Inputs from the filings (10-Q, 30 Jun 2026):

  • Backlog: $103.7bn, of which 41% is recognized in months 1-24, 39% in months 25-48 and 20% in months 49-78. Customers have prepaid $9.7bn of it, which brings no new cash.
  • Cash costs: 41.4% of revenue (the Q2 adjusted EBITDA margin was 58.6%).
  • Capacity: $52.6bn of gross equipment and construction in progress supports about $16bn a year of revenue at 3.2x capex per dollar of revenue. That ratio comes from 2026 quarterly revenue against in-service equipment: 3.0x to 3.5x, depending on how far revenue lags new equipment. The backlog peaks at $21bn a year, so the rest needs the $9.5bn of undrawn term loans, at the 63% loan-to-value observed at the SPVs.
  • Secured debt paydown: per the 10-Q maturity table, $15bn of the $18.9bn of term loans, equipment financing and Magnetar loan is repaid by end-2028.
  • Leases: bankruptcy law caps a rejected lease’s claim at the greater of one year’s rent or 15% of remaining rent, up to three years’ rent. On the $35.5bn of signed leases not yet started, that is a $5.3bn claim if all are rejected.
  • Agency conventions: 5% administrative costs, revolver 85% drawn, six months of prepetition interest.

Market inputs (indicative): compute equipment is worth 10-35% of cost after five years, per used-GPU resale estimates. H100 rents roughly halved from early 2024 to mid-2026, which sets the 50% repricing stress.

5. Is the debt cheap relative to the equity? Three tests #

5.1 Equity tail vs bonds: supports the view #

A deep out-of-the-money put pays roughly its strike if the company defaults and the stock goes to near zero. The price of a $5/$10 put spread divided by $5 is therefore a market price for “$1 paid at default before expiry” (Carr and Wu, 2011). The $5 put alone gives the low end of the range; the spread gives the high end, because it also pays when the stock merely trades between $5 and $10. Bonds and CDS give the same quantity through a hazard rate fitted to the 22 Oct prices at a chosen recovery.

  • The gap closes only at about 10% recovery. The derived range of 24-40% is well above that.
  • CDS confirms it. A flat hazard fitted to Markit’s 1,140.6bp reproduces LSEG’s 63.1% five-year default probability (62.8%) and its risk annuity (3.00). It also comes within 0.6 points of the 19.8% upfront.
  • Bond-CDS basis. On a hazard basis at 40% recovery, CDS trades 29bp wider than the 9.75% 2031s, 57bp wider than the 9.00% 2031s (the CDS reference bond), 66bp wider than the 2032s and 112bp wider than the 2030s. The notes are slightly rich to CDS, and the 9.75% 2031s are the least rich.
  • The curve may slope upward, which narrows the edge. LSEG shows only the 5-year CDS point. The shortest notes (2030s) price less default than the 2031s and trade furthest inside CDS. Using their hazard instead, credit prices 29% default by January 2029 at 34% recovery. $5 puts then cost 65% and $10 puts 78% of what the notes pay. At the low-end 24% recovery those become 75% and 89%.

5.2 Structural model: does not support the view #

The model is CreditGrades (RiskMetrics, 2002), with standard barrier settings (mean recovery 0.5, uncertainty 0.3), $76.42 of debt per share and the 85% long-dated vol:

  • At the derived 34% recovery the model prices the 9.75% 2031s 2.8 points below the market, so the notes are slightly rich. The equity vol that would make them fair is 80%, against 85% in the options market.
  • At the 40% convention the model’s 5-year CDS is 1,101bp against Markit’s 1,141bp, which is close to fair.
  • Adding leases to debt ($106/share) makes the notes look richer still.

The model is blunt rather than wrong. At 85% vol it puts a 35% default probability on January 2029, more than the bonds (31%) and the puts (19-26%). Listed options price a wide middle but a thinner default tail than a lognormal structural model does. The relative value is in the tail, not in the at-the-money vol.

5.4 Synthesis #

Sell default insurance where it is dear, by owning the notes. Buy it back where it is cheap, through long-dated puts. Keep only a modest stock short. The plain long-bond/short-stock spread trade has no edge at today’s inputs.

8. Risks and what to do about them #

  1. Recovery. The derived recovery is 34%, 24-40% across scenario weightings, and 0-5% in an AI-compute bust. The hedge is sized at 24%, so below that the position loses about $10k per $1mm face for each point of recovery, −$140k at 10%. The bust scenario is where this bites.
  2. Credit-only selloffs. New secured debt ranking ahead, a distressed exchange that favors some creditors, rating downgrades, or high-yield fund outflows can hit the notes without moving the stock. The widely cited precedent is GM in May 2005: its debt was cut to junk the day after Kerkorian’s tender offer lifted the stock, so long-debt, short-stock positions lost on both legs. CoreWeave also sits in the CDX High Yield index, so index flows can move its CDS without any stock news. Keep the stock short small and use the exit triggers in section 10.
  3. More debt before default. Each extra $5bn of unsecured debt issued before a default cuts recovery by about 4-5 points (section 4). CoreWeave has issued over $10bn of unsecured debt in 2026 alone.
  4. Distress without default. The stock drifts to $10-20 while the notes trade at 50-65. The $20 puts and the rebalancing rule cover this. The note’s stock sensitivity rises from 0.42 at $54 to about 0.9 at $20.
  5. Maturity mismatch. The puts expire in January 2029 and the notes mature in October 2031. Plan to roll or exit by mid-2028.
  6. Model risk. The note-to-stock mapping and hedge ratios come from a calibrated structural model, and the recovery derivation rests on scenario weights. Realized co-movement has been lower than the model.
  7. Access and liquidity. The notes are unregistered (Rule 144A and Regulation S lines only), and bid-ask spreads widen in stress. The January 2029 $10 puts have 1,559 contracts open, so $1mm face already uses about 20% of that. Several $1mm of face would need OTC puts.
  8. Equity squeeze or takeover. The short is small. A takeover by a stronger buyer should lift the notes toward par, and qualifying events trigger a 101 put (the indenture defines which). The note gain would outweigh the short and the lost put premium.
  9. Rates. Modified duration is 3.6, about 2.9 points per 100bp. Hedge with five-year Treasury futures if that matters at your size.

10. Running the position #

  • Rebalance the stock short to about half the model delta, per $1mm face:

    Stock price $90 $70 $54 $45 $35 $25 $20
    Target short (shares) 1,200 1,600 2,100 2,500 3,100 3,900 4,400

    Rebalance when the target moves more than 20%, or weekly.

  • Keep the default hedge sized at 24% recovery. Recompute default exposure (note price + accrued − 24) monthly. After a rally in the notes the exposure grows, and puts are cheaper then.

  • Refresh the recovery after each quarterly filing with the new backlog, debt and lease figures.

  • Roll part of the puts into longer expiries as they list. Do not hold the notes unhedged past January 2029.

  • Take profit when the notes’ default probability to January 2029 falls to the puts’ level (notes near 90 dirty at today’s tail pricing), or when the notes reach the mid-90s. The 101 change-of-control put and the assumed 104.875 first call cap the upside.

  • Stop and reassess if any of these happen:

    • the notes fall 8-10 points with the stock flat;
    • a large new secured or priority facility is announced, or assets move to unrestricted subsidiaries;
    • put-implied default probability rises above the bonds’ (the edge is gone);
    • a filing cuts the derived recovery below about 24%.

11. Converts vs straight notes: no short-convert leg #

The three converts rank equally with the notes. Priced at the notes’ credit risk, they should match the market if the convert and straight-bond markets agree. The table compares FINRA’s 23 Oct last trades with a jump-to-default binomial model at the notes’ hazard and the derived 34% recovery:

The rising-hazard version scales today’s hazard by today’s stock price over the node’s stock price, to the power 1 or 2, with diffusive vol of 45-59%.

  • The converts are cheap, not rich. Even allowing for default risk that rises as the stock falls, the 2031s screen 2-4 points cheap, the 2032s 4-6 and the 2033s 6-8. Shorting converts against the straights does not work.
  • The cheapness points toward classic convertible arbitrage: long the converts, short the stock. That is a different trade (long volatility, with a large equity delta), and gaps measured on single last-trade prints need live two-way quotes and a full convertible model before acting.

12. Implementation #

  • The notes are unregistered. The April 2031 add-on and the June 2032 notes were offered to qualified institutional buyers under Rule 144A or to non-US persons under Regulation S (pricing releases, 8-Ks of 21 Apr and 11 Jun 2026). Regulation S status follows residence, not citizenship, so a US-resident account generally cannot buy either line. A non-US account may reach the Regulation S line through a broker that carries it; check the ISIN and the minimum denomination. Otherwise treat this as a paper or institutional trade.
  • Institutional substitute for the notes: sell five-year CDS protection at Markit’s 1,141bp par spread (500bp running plus 19.8 points upfront). CDS trades 29-66bp wider than the 2031-2032 notes, so where CDS is tradeable it is the cheaper way to own the same credit risk, with no funding.
  • What LSEG Workspace shows: the Markit 5-year point only. The page states that the full curve needs Markit’s paid end-of-day service, and Refinitiv end-of-day CDS pricing is empty for this name.
  • Data to watch: CDS on LSEG’s CDSOV page, bond and convert last trades on FINRA’s bond pages, ratings and outlooks, and the Q3 filing when it lands.

13. What would change the view #

  • A steep CDS curve, with 2-year far below 5-year. A flat hazard would then overstate credit’s near-term default pricing and shrink the gap to the January 2029 puts. The full curve needs Markit’s paid end-of-day service. The bond curve hints at a mild upward slope (section 5.1), which narrows the edge. A broker run or a Bloomberg terminal with the 1-3 year points would settle it.
  • A lower derived recovery. The gap to the puts survives down to about 10% recovery. A filing that shows more secured debt, more unsecured issuance, a shorter backlog or more customer concentration would push the derivation toward the bust case.
  • Low-strike puts getting bid up until the puts imply as much default as the bonds.
  • A Q3 report showing a funding gap, with cash burn ahead of committed financing, or trouble with a major customer contract.
