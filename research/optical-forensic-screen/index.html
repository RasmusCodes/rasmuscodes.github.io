Forensic screen: AAOI, Coherent and Lumentum

AAOI is still the outlier, above the M-score cutoff for eight straight quarter-ends, though its Digicomm flag eased: 57% of receivables in September, down from 67%. Coherent’s cash fell further behind its earnings; Lumentum agreed to an all-stock sale to Marvell.

Updated 2026-11-12 with the September-quarter 10-Qs: AAOI’s for the quarter ended 2026-09-30 (filed 2026-11-05), COHR’s for fiscal Q1 2027 ended 2026-09-30 (filed 2026-11-04) and LITE’s for fiscal Q1 2027 ended 2026-09-26 (filed 2026-11-03). The first version, published 2026-10-28, used the June-quarter filings. Section 5 answers the checks it listed for the November prints. Two AAOI 8-Ks filed after the rerun, through 2026-11-27, are noted under “Cash, capex and dilution”.

Bottom line #

  • AAOI is still the outlier, though its main flag eased. Its M-score has now been above the −1.78 Beneish cutoff for eight straight quarter-ends (−1.06 at September). Digicomm, the cable-TV distributor at the centre of the first version, fell from 67.2% to 56.7% of receivables, and Digicomm’s receivable days shortened on every basis. Its balance still grew, from $211.0m to $234.5m, only more slowly than its sales, while other customers’ receivable days lengthened from about 84 to about 103. The cash burn grew with the build-out: free cash flow including equipment prepayments was −$1.30bn over twelve months (−$892m at June), payable days stretched from 188 to 233, and the share count is up 32% in a year; on 2026-11-17 AAOI signed a fourth at-the-market program, for up to $550m. The Dechow F-score edged above its 1.85 “substantial risk” band (1.86).
  • COHR moved closer to the cutoff, and its cash fell further behind its earnings. The M-score rose from −2.00 to −1.87, still in the grey zone just below −1.78. Operating cash flow was −$92.7m in the September quarter against $381.6m of net earnings, and turned negative over twelve months (−$59m against $963m). Inventory days rose from 187 to 211, as inventory grew 29% in the quarter against 16% revenue growth. A $269m rise in customer prepayments kept the cash gap from being wider. The mix of earnings improved: by my estimate, one-off and non-cash items are now about a quarter of trailing net income, against about half in FY2026.
  • LITE still converts earnings to cash best, and the news is the Marvell deal. Its M-score rose to −1.40, mostly from 103% revenue growth. Accruals stay low: operating cash flow was $961m against $1,178m of net income excluding the extinguishment loss over twelve months, and free cash flow was +$356m. On 2026-11-01 LITE agreed to be acquired by Marvell in an all-stock deal, at 4.5 Marvell shares per LITE share. Its largest customer reached 37% of quarterly revenue. Holders converted $236.8m of convertible notes in the quarter; LITE settled the conversions in cash and booked no gain or loss.

None of the three filed an auditor-change (Item 4.01) or non-reliance (Item 4.02) 8-K, a late-filing notice or SEC comment-letter correspondence between 2026-10-28 and 2026-11-12. None of this proves misstatement. Digicomm was still 41.9% of AAOI’s revenue in the first nine months of 2026, so its payments remain the thing to watch.

How to read the scores:

  • Beneish M (8-variable). Above −1.78 is the original “likely manipulator” cutoff, and −2.22 to −1.78 is a commonly used grey zone. The 5-variable version uses −2.22.
  • Dechow F-score. 1.0 means the average misstatement probability in the authors’ sample. The usual bands are above 1.85 for substantial risk and above 2.45 for high risk.
  • Montier C-score. Six yes/no warning signs; 5–6 is high.
  • Piotroski F. 8–9 is strong and 0–2 is weak.
  • Altman Z. Below 1.81 is distress and above 2.99 is safe. For Z’’ the bands are 1.10 and 2.60.
  • Sloan accruals. Net income minus operating cash flow over average assets. Persistently above roughly +10% predicts weaker future earnings.

“Ex-ext” means excluding gains and losses on debt extinguishment. LITE FY2026 and AAOI FY2024 booked large non-cash losses swapping convertible notes for stock, which would otherwise make accruals look artificially clean. LITE’s loss is still inside the trailing twelve months, which is why its reported M-score is −6.39.

1. AAOI (Applied Optoelectronics) #

The scores. The ex-ext trailing-twelve-month M-score crossed −1.78 at December 2024, peaked at +0.23 at June 2025 and was −1.06 at September 2026, the eighth straight quarter-end above the cutoff. At the peak, the drivers were the receivables index (DSRI 2.06), growth (SGI 1.78) and accruals (TATA 0.14). The September reading rests on growth (SGI 1.77) and asset quality (AQI 1.84), while the receivables index is back near neutral (DSRI 1.04). AQI is high because equipment prepayments sit in “other assets,” which reached $465.7m at September against $50.9m at the end of 2025.

The other screens:

  • Montier C-score was 6/6 in FY2024 and FY2025. On a trailing basis it was 5 at September, up from 4 at June.
  • Dechow F-score was 1.82 for FY2025 and peaked near 2.4 on a trailing basis in mid-2025. At September it was 1.86, just above the 1.85 “substantial” band.
  • Sloan accruals were +15.9% of average assets in FY2025, against negative readings in every earlier year. On a trailing basis they were 3.6% at September.
  • Altman Z’’ sat in the distress zone from FY2021 to FY2024. It recovered in FY2025 largely because equity raises lifted cash and book equity, and was 4.0 at September.

The Digicomm receivable. Digicomm is a cable-TV equipment distributor.

At September 2026 Digicomm owed $234.5m of AAOI’s $413.8m of receivables. Its share fell from 67.2% at June, but its balance still grew; receivables from other customers grew faster. The first version asked whether Digicomm’s receivables would convert to cash or keep growing faster than Digicomm’s sales. The September quarter’s answer:

  • Receivable days shortened. On a quarter basis, Digicomm’s receivable days were about 300 in Q1, 239 in Q2 and 196 in Q3. On a year-to-date basis they were 260 at June and 249 at September. The balance grew more slowly than Digicomm’s sales.
  • Collections did not rise. Implied collections from Digicomm were about $86m in Q3, against $92m in Q2. Implied Q3 Digicomm revenue was $110.0m, or 40.8% of the quarter.
  • Other customers paid more slowly. Their receivable days rose from about 84 to about 103.
  • Same terms, same expectation. The “longer than typical payment terms” paragraph is word for word the same as in the June 10-Q. The 10-Q repeats management’s expectation that Digicomm’s share of receivables will fall as data-center customers grow.

Management’s explanation for the terms, added after the SEC asked, is that it extended them so Digicomm holds inventory ready for cable operators’ network builds. Revenue recognized on shipments to a distributor that is carrying stock on extended credit is the classic setup for pulled-forward revenue. Collection history has been good so far, per the company. The September quarter moved the ratios the right way. The next test is whether Digicomm’s balance stops growing.

There is also a small internal inconsistency in the FY2025 10-K. It gives Digicomm’s 2024 revenue share as 34.1% in the business section and 35.1% in the notes.

Cash, capex and dilution.

  • Cash burn. Operating cash flow was −$174m in 2025 and −$106m over the twelve months to September, against −$132m at June. The September quarter alone was −$2.9m. Payables did most of the work: they rose $347m over nine months, and payable days went from 188 to 233. The 10-Q attributes this to inventory and equipment purchases.
  • Capacity spend. Capex was $777m plus $417m of equipment prepayments over the twelve months, about 28 times depreciation (22 times at June). The September quarter alone was about $476m of capex, land purchases and equipment prepayments combined; the 10-Q stopped reporting land purchases separately, so only the combined figure is exact for the quarter. Free cash flow including prepayments was −$1.30bn over the twelve months.
  • Inventory. Inventory days eased from 183 to 178, but inventory itself rose 36% in the quarter, to $378m. The quarter’s inventory-reserve adjustment was a $3.8m release, credited to cost of sales, against a $2.1m charge a year earlier. The MD&A lists lower write-off and reserve adjustments as one source of the gross-profit gain.
  • Funding. At-the-market equity raised over the twelve months was $1.77bn, up from $1.35bn at June. The second ATM program ($588m at a weighted average of $183.06 a share) finished 2026-08-17, and the third ($588m at $105.36) finished 2026-10-01. Shares outstanding were 89.9m at 2026-09-30 and 90.8m at 2026-11-02, up 32% in a year.
  • New debt. A $200m credit agreement with lenders led by East West Bank (2026-10-30), including $170m of term-loan commitments, replaced the BOKF facility.
  • Customer warrant. Amazon holds a warrant for up to 7.9m shares at $23.70. It vests with up to $4bn of purchases and is booked as a reduction of revenue: $4.1m in the first nine months of 2026, against $0.1m a year earlier, on $161.7m of linked revenue.
  • Profit and tax. Net income was $6.3m in the September quarter, against −$17.9m a year earlier. Management now says part of the US and Taiwan valuation allowance could be released within 12 months. A release would be a non-cash boost to earnings.
  • After the rerun (8-Ks). On 2026-11-06 AAOI bought the three buildings it had leased at Hightower Business Park in Houston (736,216 square feet) for $102.25m in cash. On 2026-11-17 it signed a fourth equity distribution agreement, for up to $550m of shares.

Moving to a Big Four auditor is generally a positive. The concern is the cluster: a weakness, an SEC review focused on receivables, and an auditor change, all within twelve months of a concentration build-up.

Text. AAOI’s 10-K Fog index is about 20, flat for years, so readability gives no signal. Its 10-K is the stickiest of the three, with 86% of 5-word phrases carried over from the prior year. The new risk factors in the FY2025 10-K cover the AI-driven US manufacturing build-out (overcapacity, impairment, stranded assets) and tariffs. None was added on Digicomm or receivable concentration.

2. COHR (Coherent) #

The scores. The M-score rose steadily from −2.64 in early 2025 to −2.00 at June 2026 and −1.87 at September. That is still in the grey zone, just below the −1.78 cutoff. At September the largest contributions are growth (SGI 1.31), accruals (TATA 0.05) and receivables (DSRI 1.19), plus a falling depreciation rate. In FY2026 the depreciation rate fell because property, plant and equipment jumped 60% with assets not yet in service. The other screens:

  • Montier C-score was 6/6 for FY2026 and is still 6.
  • Dechow F-score was 1.71 for FY2026 and 1.79 at September: above average, but below the 1.85 band.
  • Sloan accruals turned positive (+4.3%) in FY2026 for the first time in the series, and were 5.7% at September.
  • Piotroski F fell from 7 at June to 4 of 9 at September: trailing operating cash flow turned negative, leverage rose and the current ratio did not improve.

Where the FY2026 earnings came from. FY2026 consolidated net earnings were $786.9m and operating cash flow was $79.5m.

  • Non-operating items. Earnings include a $124.1m gain on the sale of the aerospace and defense business, a $74.0m gain on an equity investment, and a $198.7m deferred-tax benefit. Together these add up to about half of earnings.
  • Working capital. Inventory absorbed $1,183m. Inventory days went from 133 to 187 and inventory rose 80% against 22.5% revenue growth. Receivables absorbed $368m and other operating assets $408m.
  • Payables. Payables provided $749m, with payable days going from 79 to 138. Without that stretch, operating cash flow would have been negative. Payables also include about $372m of unpaid capex.
  • Funding. Free cash flow was −$1.02bn, funded by about $2.0bn of new common stock in the January–March 2026 quarter and a new $1.25bn term loan.
  • Disclosure. Management’s MD&A explains the inventory build in one sentence, “to support higher revenue growth.”

The September quarter (fiscal Q1 2027).

  • Cash against earnings. Operating cash flow was −$92.7m against net earnings of $381.6m. Trailing operating cash flow turned negative (−$59m) against $963m of trailing net income.
  • Customer prepayments. Contract liabilities rose $269m in the quarter, to $333m. Without them, the quarter’s operating cash flow would have been about −$362m.
  • Inventory. Inventory rose $760m in one quarter, to $3.34bn: 29% quarter on quarter against 16% revenue growth. Work in progress is $2.01bn of it. The MD&A explanation is still one line: inventory built “to support higher revenue growth”.
  • Payables. Payable days went from 138 to 159. Unpaid capex inside payables rose to $528m from about $372m. Excluding it, payable days still rose, from about 111 to about 125.
  • Funding. Free cash flow was −$1.83bn over twelve months, and capex was $776m in the quarter. The gap was funded by $773m of new borrowing under foreign-subsidiary credit lines (the August 2026 facility: $598m drawn) and $625m of short-term investment maturities.
  • Earnings mix. The $115m gain on the aerospace and defense sale, booked in fiscal Q1 2026, has rolled out of the trailing figures. Fiscal Q1 2027 had no sale or investment gains, but did include a $56m deferred-tax benefit. By my estimate, one-off and non-cash items are now about a quarter of trailing net income, against about half in FY2026.

SEC and audit. COHR went through a five-letter SEC review from April to December 2024 over non-GAAP add-backs. In December 2024 it agreed to stop excluding inventory write-offs, overlapping labor and travel, and the manufacturing inefficiencies of closing sites. EY has been auditor since 2008 and its opinion is clean. The only critical audit matter is goodwill impairment for the Lasers unit. The FY2026 10-K added a generic internal-control risk factor, but no control deficiency is disclosed, and the September 10-Q reports controls unchanged. That 10-Q gives no inventory write-off or reserve figures. The earnings release’s non-GAAP gross-margin bridge contains only stock compensation, intangible amortization and $0.4m of integration costs, with no inventory add-back. The 10-Q also discloses a BIS inquiry, received in January 2025, into past sales to Huawei; COHR says it has stopped shipping to Huawei. The inquiry is not new, but the first version of this note missed it.

Text. Negative words in COHR’s MD&A rose every year, from 0.66% in FY2021 to 1.65% in FY2026, even as earnings rose. Its risk-factor Fog is the highest of the three and rising (22.4). The MD&A was heavily rewritten: only 51% of 5-word phrases carried over from FY2025. New risk paragraphs focus on demand cancellations, excess or obsolete inventory if forecast orders do not materialize, contract-manufacturer qualification, indium phosphide supply and tariffs. That is consistent with the inventory build above.

3. LITE (Lumentum) #

The $7.76bn loss. In April and May 2026, LITE swapped $1.12bn of 2026, 2028 and 2029 convertible notes for 10.6m shares in private exchanges. The conversion value above principal was booked as a $7,756.6m non-cash, non-deductible loss. Reported FY2026 net income was −$6.94bn and the reported M-score −6.58. Both are artifacts. Excluding the loss, FY2026 net income is $822m, of which $236m is a valuation-allowance release.

Conversions since then. In fiscal Q1 2027, holders converted $236.8m of principal. LITE paid $493.3m in cash for these conversions, and the $257.0m of conversion value above principal went through paid-in capital. Under ASC 470-20 that produced no gain or loss. Further conversion requests had reached $779.9m of principal by 2026-10-30, and all the notes remain convertible. The cost now shows up as cash outflow rather than as a P&L loss: cash fell from $2.04bn to $1.27bn in the quarter, which also reflects $307m of RSU withholding taxes.

The scores, ex-ext. The M-score was −1.40 at September (−1.62 at June), further above the cutoff, but the decomposition shows why. Sales growth of 103% adds +0.92, while receivables add nothing (DSRI 1.00) and gross margin expanded (GMI 0.67). Accruals add more than at June (+0.14 against +0.04) but remain low. This is the model’s well-known false positive for hypergrowth. The other screens:

  • Dechow F-score rose from 0.83 to 1.17, near the sample average.
  • C-score is 5, up from 4.
  • Piotroski F is 7.
  • Sloan accruals rose from 1.2% to 3.6%.
  • Cash flow. Trailing operating cash flow is $961m and free cash flow +$356m. In the September quarter, operating cash flow was $267.7m against net income of $360.3m. Prepayments and other assets absorbed $114m, and income taxes $79m.
  • Working capital. Receivable days fell to 45 and inventory days held at 119. Payable days rose to 109, with unpaid capex in payables at $254m.

Other items.

  • Marvell merger. On 2026-11-01 LITE agreed to be acquired by Marvell in an all-stock deal, at 4.5 Marvell shares per LITE share. The termination fees are $2.9bn payable by LITE and $5.8bn payable by Marvell, plus a $3.2bn regulatory fee payable by Marvell.
  • NVIDIA preferred. NVIDIA bought $2.0bn of Series A convertible preferred on 2026-03-02 at $695.31 per share, and it is still outstanding. The merger agreement converts it into Marvell common stock, or into a Marvell Series B preferred with the same terms if NVIDIA elects that.
  • Customers. LITE’s two largest end customers are unnamed, at 26.6% and 15.0% of FY2026 revenue. One end customer was 37% of September-quarter revenue. One customer is 33.4% of gross receivables, up from 30.4% at June and 13.2% a year before that; two others are 13.7% and 11.3%.
  • Export-control inquiry. In August 2024 LITE received BIS and DOJ subpoenas concerning past Huawei shipments. The September 10-Q repeats the disclosure unchanged: LITE is cooperating, and the outcome cannot be predicted.
  • Executive pay. An 8-K on 2026-11-02 (Item 5.02) reports a Section 280G excise-tax reimbursement for the CEO, with approval to extend it to other named executive officers. It is not a CFO or chief accounting officer change.
  • Accounting leadership. The chief accounting officer stepped down in February 2026 ahead of retirement. onsemi’s CFO, Thad Trent, joined the board and audit committee in December 2025.
  • SEC reviews. The 2024 review covered non-GAAP labelling and MD&A quantification, and the 2025 review covered segment disclosures. Both were routine.
  • Auditor. Deloitte, auditor since 2016. The only critical audit matter is inventory valuation.

Text. Fog is stable at about 20.4 for the whole 10-K. The MD&A reads at 20.8, its highest in the series. Uncertainty words in the MD&A rose from 1.16% to 1.72% of words over five years.

4. Readability, tone and text change across the three #

These tests use the annual reports (10-Ks), so the September 10-Qs do not change them.

  • Fog index. Every 10-K since FY2021 scores 20.0–20.9 on the whole document, the normal range for 10-Ks, and no company’s readability moved enough to matter. Li (2008) links harder-to-read reports to lower and less persistent earnings, but the spreads here are a fraction of a grade level.
  • Tone (Loughran-McDonald word lists). COHR’s MD&A has turned steadily more negative. LITE’s has become more uncertain. AAOI’s has barely changed.
  • Year-over-year change (“Lazy Prices”). COHR rewrites the most, with 51% MD&A carry-over in FY2026, and AAOI the least, with 87%. In the Lazy Prices research, large changes predict weaker returns. For AAOI, the absence of change while the business transformed is itself a gap in disclosure.
  • Benford’s law. Tagged amounts in each 10-K mostly fit first-digit expectations. LITE FY2024 is the exception, with too many leading 1s (p = 0.0004), and LITE FY2025 is marginal (p = 0.017). That is consistent with many related balances near $1bn, and the test is weak at about 500 correlated numbers, so treat it as noise.

5. What the September-quarter filings showed #

The first version listed what to check in the November prints. The answers:

  • AAOI.
    • Digicomm’s share of receivables fell from 67.2% to 56.7%. Its share of revenue was roughly flat: 41.9% for nine months, against 42.8% for the first half.
    • Digicomm’s receivable days shortened on every basis, from about 239 to 196 days on a quarter basis.
    • Operating cash flow was −$106m over twelve months, against −$132m at June, helped mainly by payables.

  • COHR.
    • Inventory days rose again, from 187 to 211.
    • Payable days rose again, from 138 to 159.
    • Operating cash flow against net income without one-off gains got worse: −$92.7m against $381.6m of net earnings, in a quarter with no sale or investment gains.
    • The 10-Q gives no inventory reserve or write-off figures, and the non-GAAP bridge has no inventory add-back.
  • LITE.
    • There were no new convert exchanges. Holders converted $236.8m of principal for cash, with no gain or loss, and further conversion requests reached $779.9m.
    • Its largest customer was 33.4% of gross receivables (30.4% at June) and 37% of quarterly revenue.
    • The BIS/DOJ disclosure is unchanged.
    • New: the all-stock merger agreement with Marvell (2026-11-01).

Method and caveats #

  • Data. All figures come from SEC XBRL company facts and the filed documents. Trailing-twelve-month figures are fiscal year plus year-to-date minus the prior year-to-date. Quarter-basis working-capital days use the latest quarter’s sales or COGS × 91.25.
  • Rerun. The 2026-11-12 rerun reproduces every earlier quarter’s scores to two decimals, so all the changes above come from the new quarter. New-quarter revenue, receivables, inventory, payables and operating cash flow for all three companies were checked against the 10-Q statements.
  • Hand fixes. LITE tags “Accounts receivable, net” as a contract-asset concept, and its cash-flow change matches; since its FY2026 10-K it also reports a stray AccountsReceivableNetCurrent fact that is not the balance-sheet line, which the panel skips. LITE’s $1,999.7m preferred is carried in paid-in capital; XBRL shows only its par value. AAOI switched its capex tag in 2026.
  • Hand checks. The FY2025 AAOI, FY2026 COHR and FY2026 LITE M-scores were recomputed by hand and match. Balance sheets and cash-flow statements were checked against the filed 10-K and 10-Q text.
  • Model limits. These models were estimated on older, mostly steady-state samples. Hypergrowth (sales-growth index), capex booms (asset-quality and depreciation indices, Dechow’s broad accruals) and acquisitions all push scores toward “risk” without any misstatement. All three companies are in an AI-driven growth and capex cycle, so read the components rather than the headline score.
  • Readability. Computed in-house. Numeric tables are removed using the Loughran-McDonald 15%-digit rule. Syllables come from the Loughran-McDonald dictionary, then the CMU dictionary, then a heuristic; on a labelled test the counter matched 82 of 84 words and classified every complex word correctly. Absolute Fog levels can differ by about one point from other tools, but comparisons across these filings are like-for-like.
  • Gaps. A few 2020–2021 10-Qs lack tagged PP&E, so some early quarterly M-scores are missing. Insider trading, short interest, earnings-call transcripts and a full non-GAAP reconciliation were not analysed.
