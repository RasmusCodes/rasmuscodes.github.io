Three thesis points

  1. The international switch to Netflix is a decade-long trend, and today’s price gives it no credit.
    • Only about a third of broadband homes outside the US & Canada pay for Netflix (34%), against about two thirds at home (63%).
    • International members grew 6–16% a year in 2025–26.
    • Our base case lifts international penetration to 41% by 2035. At $60.75 the market implies it falls to ~27%.
  2. Pricing power, not advertising, carries the US business, and live sports protect it.
    • The US & Canada region is nearly saturated, so its growth is price.
    • Live events are Netflix’s answer to the last reason people keep cable (sports and news). They cost ~5% of content spend and drive 6 of the 10 biggest sign-up days in five years.
    • A full-season sports package would need a ~9% price increase to pay for itself. Netflix’s “events, not seasons” approach is the right one.
  3. India is the biggest long-run opportunity and the smallest near-term number.
    • Members could reach ~50M by 2035 (from ~20M), but at ~$2.50 a month India stays under 2% of revenue.
    • Prices have not moved since 2023, and JioHotstar (IPL cricket, >300M subscriptions) owns sports.
    • India is an option on future pricing and a content factory, not a reason to own the stock this year.

Key catalysts:

  • Q4-26 results and the 2027 guide (~19–20 Jan 2027; options imply ±9.8%).
  • The FY26 engagement report (Q1-27).
  • The ad plan launching in 12 new countries (March 2027).
  • The next US price increase (implied ~Q2-27).
  • The NFL Week 18 game (9 Jan 2027) and the Women’s World Cup (Jun–Jul 2027).

Principal risks:

  • YouTube taking time from Netflix faster than prices can rise.
  • International price competition, India above all.
  • Currency (EMEA and LATAM are ~44% of revenue).
  • Sports-rights inflation if Netflix starts bidding for full seasons.
  • A higher cost of capital.

What changed since version 1 (HOLD, $68, earlier the same day). The first model was detailed (~2,800 formulas). It treated the ad plan as a heavy drag on revenue per member, and it stacked a separate “AI threat” scenario on top of each operating case. We rebuilt it as a simple model you can audit in ten minutes: households × penetration × price, by region, with 19 dials. Four things changed the answer:

  1. International adoption is now explicit. Penetration rises from 34% to 41% of international broadband homes, giving 508M members by 2035 (version 1: 458M).
  2. The AI and competition risks sit inside one bear case (returns on new content fall to 15%, margins stay flat) instead of a separate 25%-probability dimension.
  3. Margins and US pricing follow the evidence more closely.
    • Content falls to 29% of revenue and other costs to 28% by 2035, a 43% margin. That is still a slower climb than the Street’s (UBS 41% by 2030, Oppenheimer 38.5%).
    • US & Canada revenue per member grows 5% a year, below the ~6% a year of 2019–26.
  4. The target is the DCF value itself. It is the probability-weighted value today, not rolled forward a year at the cost of equity.

The base value moved from $65 to $73 and the target from $68 to $71. Our caution on advertising stands: the model uses all-in revenue per member, which already nets out members trading down to the cheaper ad plan.

1. Executive summary #

Netflix has fallen 55% from its June-2025 high (Exhibit 1). The worry is attention: YouTube’s share of US TV time hit 15.8% in August 2026, twice Netflix’s 7.7%. The 2026 ad target was also cut, to ~$2.7B. Meanwhile the business keeps compounding:

  • 2026: revenue +13% to $51.1B, operating margin 31.5% (from 29.5%), free cash flow ~$12B.
  • Q3-26: a record $4.8B buyback, and churn improved globally and in the US after price increases.

Investors are arguing about the wrong market. In the US & Canada, Netflix is already in ~63% of broadband homes, so the US is a pricing story. Outside it, Netflix reaches only ~34% of broadband homes, and those homes are multiplying: India’s broadband base has roughly tripled since 2019, and Latin America and Southeast Asia are still wiring up. Three secular forces push more of those homes to Netflix:

  • Broadband and smart TVs, which set the size of the market.
  • The decline of pay-TV, which frees up entertainment budgets.
  • Netflix’s local-content machine, where non-English titles are now more than a third of viewing.

Our simple model turns this into numbers. Members grow from ~352M (2026) to ~508M (2035), revenue compounds at ~7.2% to $96B, and margins expand to 43% as content grows slower than revenue. Our DCF value is $71 per share: the probability-weighted value of the bear ($43), base ($73) and bull ($97) cases. That is our price target, 18% above the price. At $60.75 the market implies international penetration falls to ~27% by 2035, from 34% today. We think that is too pessimistic, so we rate the shares BUY.

3. The international secular shift #

The evidence that the switch is still happening.

  • Members: EMEA grew from 101M (end-2024) to ~118M (end-2026E), LATAM from 53M to ~63M, and APAC from 58M to ~74M. That is 6–16% a year, far faster than the US & Canada (90M → ~97M).
  • Revenue (Q3-26, constant currency): APAC +15%, LATAM +12%, EMEA +9%. Brokers expect members to keep growing 4–8% a year in EMEA and LATAM and 8–14% in APAC through 2028 (JPM, Guggenheim).
  • Content: non-English titles are more than a third of viewing, against under 10% a decade ago. Local hits from Japan, Korea, Mexico, Denmark, the Netherlands and India travel across borders.
  • Headroom: brokers estimate Netflix has reached under 45% of the broadband homes it could serve and holds ~5% of global TV time (JPMorgan).

Why it continues.

  • Broadband keeps spreading. Fixed broadband subscriptions grew ~19% a year in India (2019–24) and ~3% a year in Latin America. Fibre and fixed-wireless are reaching homes that were mobile-only.
  • Pay-TV is shrinking outside the US too. India’s DTH paying base fell ~12% year on year, and Latin American pay-TV is flat at ~53M.
  • A cheap entry price. The ad plan (US $8.99, UK £7.99), mobile plans (India ₹149) and partner bundles (Flipkart in India, telcos elsewhere) lower the price barrier.
  • Scale economics. Netflix’s ~$20B content budget, spread over ~350M members, buys a local slate no national rival can match, and each hit can then travel abroad.

Why it might not.

  • Price. International ARM is a fraction of the US figure ($12 in EMEA, $9 in LATAM and APAC ex-India, $2.45 in India), and currency weakness erodes it in dollars.
  • Engagement. Hours per member fell from 2.2 to 1.6 a day between 2023 and 2026 (UBS), as Netflix added lighter viewers.
  • Local competition. Free YouTube is strong everywhere, and local sports-led platforms (JioHotstar, DAZN, Globoplay) compete hard.

The pattern is consistent. Where Netflix is saturated it grows through price, and where it is under-penetrated it grows through members at low prices. The international thesis is therefore a member thesis. It raises revenue less than member counts suggest: global ARM grows only ~2.7% a year in our base case, below the ~3.6% revenue-weighted average of the regions’ own ARM growth, because low-price regions grow fastest (Exhibit 3).

5. India: what happens next #

Today.

  • Netflix India’s revenue was ₹3,769 crore in FY25 (+32%; filing via press), roughly $0.5B a year, against ~20M members (sources range 15–25M).
  • In Q3-26 Netflix had its largest-ever quarter of Indian member additions, helped by a Flipkart loyalty bundle that gives a free mobile plan to frequent shoppers. India revenue grew ~18% in constant currency and view hours 24%.
  • Prices are unchanged since 2023 (₹149 mobile to ₹649 premium), so growth is all volume.

The structural picture.

  • The market is huge: ~200M TV homes, ~65M broadband homes (wired 47M plus fixed-wireless 18M, TRAI May-2026), 173M paid streaming subscriptions (Ormax).
  • It is also the world’s hardest to monetise. The leader, JioHotstar (Reliance and Disney’s merged platform), has more than 300M subscriptions built on IPL cricket at very low prices. YouTube is free, and piracy is common.
  • Netflix’s position is the premium global storytelling brand plus a growing local slate, sold through bundles.

Our view (Exhibit 4).

  • Members grow with broadband homes to ~49M by 2035 (bear 40M, bull 59M), but revenue reaches only ~$1.7B (bear $1.1B, bull $2.6B), under 2% of Netflix’s revenue.
  • India’s value is optionality. If incomes and connected-TV adoption lift ARM toward $5 (the bull path), India becomes a ~$3B business in the 2030s.
  • It is also a content source for the rest of Asia.
  • What would change our mind: a price increase that holds (bullish), or Netflix matching JioHotstar on price (bearish).

6. Live sports and the cable bundle #

US pay-TV has shrunk to about 62M homes (41M traditional plus 21M virtual, low-reliability 2026 estimate), from about 100M at its 2010–12 peak. MoffettNathanson sees a floor of about 58M “sports-viewing” homes. What keeps people paying ~$80–100 a month is live sports and news. The networks know it: ESPN launched a $29.99 direct-to-consumer service and FOX One $19.99 (both August 2025).

What Netflix is doing. It is buying events, not seasons:

  • NFL: Christmas Day, Thanksgiving Eve, a Week 1 game in Melbourne and a Week 18 game on 9 Jan 2027, extended through 2029-30.
  • WWE Raw: weekly, ~$5B over 10 years.
  • Baseball: MLB’s Home Run Derby and Opening Night (2026–28), and the World Baseball Classic in Japan (~¥15B; the most-watched programme ever on Netflix Japan).
  • Football and boxing: the Women’s World Cup in the US and Canada (2027 and 2031), plus boxing.

Management says live is ~5% of the content budget and ~1% of viewing hours. But live produced 6 of the 10 biggest sign-up days in five years, and in-game ad inventory sells out (NFL Christmas 2025 sold out more than four months ahead).

The economics (Exhibit 5). A full-season national package costs on the order of $2B a year. To pay that back from US & Canada members alone, Netflix would need either a $1.71 a month price increase on every member (+8.8%) or 10.8M extra members (+11%). If neither came through, the events would have to generate about $2.5B a year of advertising on their own.

Events capture the “can’t miss” value of sports at a fraction of a season’s cost. Full seasons would turn Netflix into a sports-rights bidder against Amazon, YouTube, ESPN and the networks, which is the business that made cable expensive.

News and daily sports. Netflix will not build news. The TF1 partnership in France (launched June 2026: five live channels, including news and sports, inside the Netflix app) shows how to aggregate the cable “glue” without paying for rights. Morgan Stanley estimates that hosting paid third-party channels such as FOX One at a ~15% take could add ~$1.2B of revenue for every 20% share of the ~$40B non-Netflix US premium streaming market. That is not in our base case; it is upside.

How it enters the model. Live sits inside content cost (29–35% of revenue). Its benefit sits in US & Canada penetration (63% → 66%) and pricing (+5%/yr). We do not add a separate sports revenue line, to avoid double counting.

7. What the sell-side and buy-side expect, and where we differ #

Sell-side.

  • 33 of 51 analysts rate the stock Buy, 16 Hold and 2 Sell. The mean target is $85 (median $82, range $57–126). Most cut targets in October as the 2027 debate intensified.
  • Consensus EPS is $3.77 for 2027 and $4.50 for 2028.
  • The bulls (JPM, MS, UBS, DB, Guggenheim, Evercore) argue double-digit revenue growth with modest engagement, a new content chief and sports upside.
  • The bears (Wells Fargo $57, HSBC, Needham, Barclays) argue falling hours per member, YouTube and single-digit 2027 growth.
  • Brokers model members at ~348–355M for 2026 and 382–402M for 2028. Nobody models households or penetration by region, and nobody puts a dollar value on India.

Buy-side (Exhibit 6).

  • The stock trades at 16x 2027 EPS and 19.9x trailing earnings, the 2nd percentile of its history.
  • In Q3-26, 13F filings show growth funds selling: Fidelity −49%, Wellington −75%, Baillie Gifford −46%.
  • Value and multi-manager funds bought: Harris/Oakmark +73%, GMO +32%, Pershing Square +18% to 15.4M shares, Two Sigma.
  • Short interest rose to 92M shares, and options imply ±9.8% for January.

9. Stock behaviour #

Earnings days. They carry ~28% of NFLX’s return variance on 1.6% of trading days. The average absolute reaction is 8.2%, and 28 of 44 were negative (Exhibit 12).

Between prints. The stock is not flat: inter-earnings volatility is ~36% a year, twice the S&P 500, and the median cycle ends 12% from where it began. Options offered no reliable edge after spreads. The full study is Does Netflix trade flat between earnings?

Implication. Build the position before or after the January print rather than through it, and size it for a ±10% event move.

11. Red team and what would change our mind #

  1. The bear has a point on engagement. Falling hours per member could eventually cap price increases. We hold the US & Canada at +5% a year, below the ~6% of 2019–26 and the ~7% of 2026.
  2. Our household data are rough. Broadband subscriptions include business lines, members are not households, and the India ratio is inflated by mobile-only plans. The level of penetration is approximate. Its direction and the headroom are robust.
  3. We are below the Street on 2027–28 EPS. The BUY rests on the long run. If 2027 guidance disappoints, the stock can fall first.
  4. The BUY clears our 15% hurdle by under 3 points. With a 41% margin in 2035 and US pricing at +4% a year (our first version-2 settings), the target would be $69 (+13%), a HOLD. The rating therefore depends on Netflix keeping its pricing cadence and lifting margins about 1.3 points a year, slower than the Street expects.

We would downgrade on:

  • two years of international member growth below ~3%;
  • a failed US price increase;
  • Netflix bidding for a full-season sports package without a matching price increase.

12. Conclusion #

Netflix is a global subscription utility for entertainment. It is in about two thirds of US & Canada broadband homes and about a third of the rest of the world’s, and it is cheaper per hour than any rival. The market has priced it as if international adoption were over. We think the switch from pay-TV and piracy to paid streaming has years to run outside the US, that live events protect US pricing at a fraction of cable’s cost, and that India is a long-dated option. BUY, 12-month target $71, the probability-weighted DCF value.

About the model #

The model is an Excel workbook in six sheets:

  • Summary: rating, DCF value by case, price target, reverse DCF, financial summary vs consensus, WACC × terminal-growth sensitivity, live-sports break-even and model checks (all pass).
  • Assumptions: every input, with the 19 dials in Bear / Base / Bull columns and the evidence for each.
  • Base Case, Bear Case, Bull Case: identical layouts running revenue build → income statement → free cash flow → DCF.
  • Reverse DCF: the base case with 2035 international penetration solved so that the value equals the share price.

There is no scenario switch: every case is calculated on its own sheet. The penetration, revenue-per-member and content sensitivities in this memo come from a Python version of the model that reproduces the workbook to the cent. The detailed version-1 model and memo are archived.

Facts, estimates and opinions.

  • Revenue, margins, cash flow, guidance and prices are reported or market data.
  • Members after 2024 are broker estimates.
  • Households are World Bank/ITU and TRAI data with our growth rates.
  • India subscribers and revenue come from press reports of filings.
  • All 2035 dials are our judgement, with the evidence noted beside each dial in the model.
