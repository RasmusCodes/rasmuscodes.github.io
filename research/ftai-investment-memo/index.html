The call. We are short FTAI. On our cash-flow DCF the base case is worth $120 a share (−28%). Weighting the bear, base and bull cases 30/50/20 gives $138 (−17%). The 12-month probability-weighted value, accreting at the cost of equity, is $152 (−9% before the ~1.2% dividend).

The Street sees +86–116% upside: September targets are $310–360, and all but two of the 10–11 covering analysts rate the stock Buy. That view needs management’s $2.3B 2027 plan, including $450M from a Power business that has not delivered a unit, and a 16.5–18x multiple on earnings that convert poorly to cash. The independent evidence points the other way: an old engine fleet flying 6–8% less each year, GAAP operating cash flow negative in 6 of the last 8 quarters, and gains on a shrinking leasing book worth about a third of Adjusted EBITDA. The risk is a fat right tail: our bull case ($328, +96%) is real if Power works at scale.

1. Recommendation summary #

The thesis in five points #

  1. The price already discounts consensus, and consensus is too high.
    • Run through our cash-flow DCF, the Street’s numbers (2027E Adj. EBITDA ~$2.2B, 2028E ~$2.9B) are worth $193 (+15%), close to today’s price.
    • Our evidence-based base case is −12% below consensus in 2027 (1,912 vs 2,181) and −18% below in 2028 (2,338 vs 2,868).
    • Most of the 2027 gap is FTAI Power: our base is $214M against the $450M guide. The dedicated Power study’s base is $440M on the same ~$7–8M of EBITDA per turbine; the difference is 2027 volume (45 vs 61 turbines) and opex, and the evidence on production leans to the lower figure.
    • After 2027 the gap is also module growth, which needs share gains in a CFM56 shop-visit market that the OEMs see flat to 2028 and falling after.
  2. EBITDA is not cash.
    • GAAP operating cash flow was −$188M in 2024, −$311M in 2025 and −$265M in H1’26, while Adjusted EBITDA reached $1.19B (2025).
    • The gap was funded by selling the leasing book: $1.71B of proceeds in 2025 and $0.79B in H1’26. That book has fallen from $2.37B to $1.15B in 18 months.
    • About a third of Adjusted EBITDA has equalled gains on those asset sales.
    • Another $219M (2027E) is FTAI’s non-cash share of its funds’ and JV’s EBITDA.
    • Our base-case unlevered FCF after stock comp is $692M in 2027 against $1,912M of EBITDA (36% conversion), rising to 61% by 2030.
  3. The core market is ageing in plain sight.
    • US carriers flew their 737NG fleet 6.5% fewer hours and their A320ceo family 7.5% fewer in H1’26 (BTS T-100). The CFM56/V2500 share of US narrowbody hours is 64% (Jun-26), against 97% in 2019.
    • Repair dollars still rise (+18% y/y) because the fleet is older. That supports 2026–27 volumes, but it is a pool that shrinks after 2028.
    • FTAI’s Aerospace margin has fallen ~10 points in two years, and the 40% target set in February was withdrawn in July.
  4. FTAI Power is real, but FTAI does not control it.
    • The $1.465B order belongs to J&F Power Systems, which Jereh consolidates.
    • Units, transfer price, JV share and customer are all undisclosed.
    • First delivery has slipped from “by Q4 2026” to 2027.
    • Executive Order 14421 lists generation turbines, and DOE rules are due ~24 Dec.
    • The closest comparable (Boom’s 1.21 GW order from Crusoe) was cancelled on 25 Sep.
  5. Disclosure and governance add to the discount, not subtract from it.
    • 20–30% of Aerospace revenue is sold to a fund FTAI manages and part-owns, in which executives hold carried interest.
    • Two 2026 guidance items have already been contradicted (40% AP margin; $575M Leasing).
    • None of the Power JV, the order or EO 14421 appears in the 10-Q.
    • The CFO resigned in March. Insiders sold ~$64.6M and bought ~$3.3M since 2025.

What the price implies #

Holding everything else at our base case, $167 needs one of the following:

  • terminal growth of 4.4% after 2035, for a CFM56-centred business;
  • a 7.9% WACC, against a 1.57 regression beta;
  • 177 Power turbines in 2028 (~202 by 2030; base 70/80);
  • an Aerospace EBITDA margin of 40.7%;
  • 2,432 modules in 2028 (base 1,700).

None of these is impossible on its own. The Street case needs several at once.

Why now: the next 90 days #

  • Q3’26 results (late Oct – early Nov).
    • Our base Adj. EBITDA is $316M, against $332M consensus and $302M at Jefferies.
    • We watch for a GAAP cash-flow turn, inventory below $1.7B, and the first disclosure of the J&F stake.
  • DOE rules under EO 14421 (~24 Dec). A binary event for the Power narrative.
  • First Mod-1 customer delivery (early 2027). A miss pushes 2027 Power units well below 60.

What would make us wrong (and flip us to long) #

  • A second Power order from a customer independent of Jereh, with disclosed units and economics.
  • On-time commissioning with published output, heat rate and NOx.
  • DOE excluding behind-the-meter gensets.
  • Aerospace margin back above ~32% with module growth above 1,700 in 2027.
  • GAAP operating cash flow turning sustainably positive without relying on asset sales.

Any two of these together would move our probability weights toward the bull case. The bull probability that makes the expected value equal to the price is 32%, against our 20%.

[!warn] Risks to the short. The stock is already down 48% from its high, and all but two of the 10–11 covering analysts rate it Buy. Short interest is 5.9% of float (4.4 days to cover). A $500M buyback is authorised. The bull case is +96%. Shorts in this name face sharp squeezes on Power headlines. The right tail is the reason conviction is moderate rather than high.

Why we put the DCF first:

  • FTAI’s Adjusted EBITDA contains items a multiple overvalues:
    • FTAI’s pro-rata share of its funds’ EBITDA. That EBITDA services fund-level debt FTAI does not carry, and FTAI receives only distributions.
    • Gains on a leasing book that is running off.
    • Insurance recoveries.
  • Its core asset base (CFM56/V2500 engines) has a finite life. A forward multiple treats both the EBITDA and the asset base as if they persisted. The DCF does not.

Where we differ:

  • 2026: no disagreement. Our $1,290M is within 3% of consensus ($1,322M).
  • 2027: Power and leasing. We have 45 turbines, not ~60. We charge the Power R&D run-rate (~$90M) to the Power segment. FTAI’s share of J&F’s margin arrives a quarter after the turbine sale.
  • 2028 onward: volume. We do not model 25% market share. Our modules grow 1,500 → 1,700 → 1,850 (2027–30), against the Street’s ~1,850–2,000 by 2028. Power holds at 70–80 turbines a year rather than scaling past 100.

5. Aerospace Products: the growth is real; its quality and durability are the question #

5.1 Demand: an older fleet that flies less but costs more to keep flying #

  • Hours are falling, and faster.
    • US 737NG block hours −6.5% y/y in H1’26 and A320ceo −7.5% (June −10.6%).
    • Aircraft in service: 737NG −2.7% and A320ceo −5.2% y/y (Q2’26, BTS Form 41 P-5.2).
    • Jet fuel at $4.37/gal (Sep-26, +109% y/y) punishes the thirstier ceo/NG fleet.
  • Dollars are rising.
    • US carriers’ outside engine-repair spend on these fleets rose 18% y/y in H1’26.
    • 737NG engine maintenance cost per airborne hour was $422, against $235 in 2019.
    • The fleet is ageing into heavier shop visits. This is why FTAI’s 2026 volumes are well supported.
  • Shop visits have peaked.
    • GE and Safran guide CFM56 visits at ~2,300–2,400 a year to 2027–28, with Safran expecting declines from 2029. V2500 is just over 800 a year.
    • Retirements are low (GE: 1.5–2% in 2026). That keeps visits up but makes feedstock dear.
  • The replacement engines are getting fixed. GTF groundings are past their peak, and the LEAP-1B durability kit was certified in Jul-26. Both reduce the need to keep old aircraft flying in 2027–28.

5.2 FTAI is growing through share and related-party sales, not the market #

  • Modules and share.
    • Modules: 757 (FY25) → 270 (Q1’26) → 296 (Q2’26); ~1,180–1,200 is on track for 2026.
    • FTAI claims ~14% share, with 25% as its goal. Excluding sales to its own SCI fund, the revenue-based share is ~11%. On modules (≈3 per shop visit), the Q2’26 run-rate is 12–13% of CFM56 + V2500 visits.
    • The 25% goal needs ~2,360 modules a year in a flat market. Management’s 2027 goal is 1,700.
  • Related-party revenue.
    • “MRE contract revenue” — engines and modules sold mostly to the 2025 Partnership (SCI I), which FTAI services and ~19–20% owns — was $335.8M in 2025 (17% of AP) and $404.0M in H1’26 (25%).
    • Segment EBITDA keeps 100% of the margin; only FTAI’s share is eliminated.
  • Capacity.
    • Hiring is accelerating modestly: 103 open postings on 3 October, ~14–17 new requisitions a month in the US.
    • Partner shops have been added (GMF Indonesia, now a five-year deal; EgyptAir).
    • Satellite imagery shows a new building next to the Montreal plant (May-23 → May-25; ownership not established). Every latest capture predates the 2026 ramp.

5.3 Unit economics: price up, cost up faster #

Why the margin fell:

  • Heavier workscopes, according to management.
  • Feedstock bought at market prices, replacing low-basis engines transferred from the leasing book. This is the mechanism Muddy Waters highlighted in 2025.
  • Pricing for share.

The industry pricing umbrella is rising (+24% y/y per flight hour on the 737NG), so FTAI is not capturing it. Our base holds ~30% (“around 30% for 1–2 years”, Jul-26 call). The bear case lets it slide to 26% by 2030.

6. FTAI Power: real demand, an unproven product, and economics FTAI does not control #

6.1 What is established #

  • Product.
    • Mod-1: a ~25 MW aeroderivative turbine built on a remanufactured CFM56 core, launched on 30 Dec 2025.
    • Tested in Montreal from January to May 2026, then on a genset in Miami.
    • No full-load, heat-rate or NOx data have been published, and there is no SCR at launch.
  • Structure.
    • FTAI sells the turbine to J&F Power Systems LLC, which Jereh (002353.SZ) calls its controlled subsidiary and consolidates.
    • J&F packages the genset (Jereh supplies generator, gearbox, trailer and controls) and sells it.
    • FTAI books turbine revenue on sale to J&F, and equity income later, when J&F sells.
  • The order.
    • $1.465B of gensets for an undisclosed “international cloud service provider”, equal to 61% of Jereh’s 2025 revenue.
    • Five-year master supply agreement; deliveries in batches before Nov-2027; advance received by 13 Aug.
    • Terms: price cuts of up to 10% for low output; delay damages capped at 10%; final payment after commissioning.
    • The customer had already bought $254.8M of Jereh gensets since Nov-2025, before the JV existed.
  • Disclosure.
    • Neither the order nor the JV appears in FTAI’s 10-Q, and there was no 8-K.
    • The Q2’26 10-Q was filed nine days after the order and does not mention Jereh, J&F or the order.
  • Guidance. $450M of 2027 Power EBITDA, the low end of a $450–750M range. The guide assumes “materially less” than 100 units. First delivery has moved from “by Q4 2026” (Feb) to “conservative to expect 2027” (Jul).

The two independent studies agree on per-unit economics: ~$7–8M of EBITDA per turbine, close to the ~$7.5M that $450M ÷ 60 implies. They disagree on volume and opex. Both questions get answered within months: the unit count (if disclosed), the first commissioning, and the delivery cadence.

What $450M requires, at our per-unit economics:

  • ~76 units in 2027; or
  • $12M of EBITDA per unit at 45 units; or
  • a ~$16.8M transfer price, ~69% of the implied genset price.

The Power study shows the same tension a second way. At margins “in line with Aerospace” (30–40%), $450M needs ~77–88 units unless the turbine captures 70–80% of the package value. None of this is contradicted, and none of it is supported by disclosed data.

6.3 The market: demand is not the problem, technology choice and policy are #

  • Demand.
    • LBNL: US data-center electricity 192 TWh (2024) → 464 TWh (2028) → 649 TWh (2030), needing ~148 GW of interconnection by 2030.
    • ERCOT’s large-load queue is ~474 GW against 5.9 GW energised, and ERCOT paused energisation of ≥75 MW data centers pending an audit.
    • PJM capacity auctions clear at the cap ($325–333/MW-day), 6.5–6.8 GW short.
    • Our central estimate is ~28 GW (range 15–50) of data-center demand that cannot get grid power on time in 2026–28.
  • Choice.
    • Named AI buyers took ~10.7 GW of reciprocating engines and ~3.3 GW of fuel cells, against ~2.7 GW of new aeroderivatives.
    • Nebius switched to fuel cells for permitting.
    • Crusoe cancelled Boom’s 1.21 GW aero-derived order and kept GE LM2500XPRESS units, used as backup where the grid is primary.
  • Economics for the customer.
    • Onsite LCOE is ~6.6–8.5¢/kWh at $3–5 gas, cheaper than PJM industrial tariffs (9–10.5¢, up 7–26% y/y). It is not cheaper than Texas, Louisiana or Oklahoma (6.4–6.7¢), where the case is speed.
    • A year of grid delay forgoes ~$1.7M of colocation lease revenue per MW (Applied Digital lease terms), many times any onsite cost premium: time-to-power is the engine of the whole category.
  • Policy.
    • EO 14421 (26 Aug) lists “generation turbines”; anything not made or assembled in the US is foreign-produced, and the order applies notwithstanding existing contracts.
    • A Chinese newspaper reports North American modules being assembled in Yantai.
    • DOE’s RFI comments are due 9 Oct; rules ~24 Dec. Jereh and Barclays expect behind-the-meter units to be exempt. That is unresolved, not excluded.
  • Permits. Uncontrolled, one 25 MW unit emits ~92 t/yr of NOx, so about three units cross the 250 t/yr major-source threshold (counted site-wide).

6.4 Production and units #

  • No independent evidence of a 100-turbine line.
    • 7 explicit Power job postings.
    • No permits, leases or test-cell additions found.
    • Jereh’s recent Houston imports are 1–20 t each, none turbine-sized.
  • The packager is stretched. Jereh is absorbing >$3.1B of data-center genset orders, ~129% of its 2025 revenue, with no visible dedicated capex.
  • Slippage has already happened before series production.

7. Aviation Leasing and Strategic Capital: from rent to gains and fund economics #

  • Running off by design.
    • Engines fell from 312 to 176 and aircraft from 109 to 22 (YE24 → Jun-26).
    • Lease income was $27.8M in Q2’26, against $62.4M a year earlier.
    • Leasing EBITDA exceeded segment revenue in 2025, because it includes:
      • gains on sales to the 2025 Partnership ($46M in 2025);
      • Russia insurance recoveries ($54M in 2025; $50M in H1’26);
      • FTAI’s pro-rata share of SCI EBITDA.
  • SCI is the growth leg.
    • SCI I: ~$2.0B of equity and >300 aircraft closed or under LOI.
    • The 2026 SPV has a $2.0B warehouse facility (Aug-26). FTAI commits ~15–19% of each vehicle’s equity, and executives hold carried interest.
    • On 28 Sep FTAI bought 27 WestJet 737-700s: 17 go to the 2026 SPV on sale-leaseback, and 10 become AP feedstock.
  • Guidance and our case. 2026 was cut from $575M to $475M (Jefferies $446M). 2027 is $450M. Our base is $455M / $430M.
  • What we flex. Fees, gains, income and distributions are the cash-relevant parts. In our model the pro-rata EBITDA is not cash. Flexing SCI economics ±25% moves value only ±$5 a share.

8. Earnings quality and cash flow #

Four facts the bull case must explain:

  1. Operating cash flow has been negative while EBITDA grew.
    • CFO was −$188M (2024), −$311M (2025) and −$265M (H1’26).
    • Inventory went from $551M to $1,545M in 18 months.
    • Engine and aircraft inventory purchases inside CFO were $325M (2025) and $357M (H1’26).
  2. Asset sales filled the gap.
    • Investing inflows that contain inventory components were $1,059M (2025) and $538M (H1’26).
    • The SEC reviewed FTAI’s cash-flow classification (closed Jan-2025) and accepted it. The classification is still FTAI’s choice, and it shapes every cash KPI.
  3. Gains are a large share of EBITDA.
    • The cash-flow add-back for gains on assets sold equalled 44% of Adj. EBITDA in 2024, 36% in 2025 and 32% in H1’26.
    • As the leasing book shrinks, either those gains fall or they must be replaced by margin on purchased feedstock.
  4. Company “free cash flow” adds asset-sale proceeds. Dividends were raised on “strong FCF” in quarters when GAAP CFO was +$5M, −$179M and −$160M.

What looks clean (balance matters):

  • Unqualified audits: EY to FY24, KPMG FY25, with ICFR effective. The auditor change in Jun-25 reported no disagreements.
  • The SEC review closed without restatement.
  • The audit committee’s forensic review found the Jan-2025 Muddy Waters allegations without merit.
  • DSO is low (16 days).
  • AP growth has continued for six quarters after the short report.

What remains unresolved:

  • Muddy Waters’ Iran report (Mar-2025): no OFAC or DOJ action found.
  • Shannahan v. FTAI (S.D.N.Y.): motion to dismiss pending.
  • The “over 1,000 engines” feedstock claim does not reconcile with 176 leasing engines plus an undisclosed inventory count.

10. Financial projections #

10.1 How the model works #

Structure. Our projection is quarterly from Q3’26 to Q4’30, in the column order of our CapIQ-based Excel model. It is built from a quarterly driver grid.

  • Aerospace: modules x revenue per module x margin.
  • Power: turbines x transfer price − (feedstock + conversion) + J&F income, lagged a quarter, − Power opex.
  • Leasing: GAAP lease, maintenance, asset-sale and fee lines, plus a Leasing Adj. EBITDA input. The difference is FTAI’s non-cash share of fund EBITDA.
  • Corporate: opex, G&A and stock comp.
  • Balance sheet: inventory on AP COGS days plus Power working capital; receivables on DSO; payables on DPO. The leasing equipment roll-forward drives D&A and the gains on sale.

Cash flow.

  • CFO = net income + D&A − gains on sale − equity income + distributions + stock comp − working-capital build.
  • CFI = −(leasing capex + deposits + PP&E + Power tooling) + sale proceeds − SCI co-investment.
  • Unlevered FCF = CFO + after-tax interest + CFI, less stock comp for valuation.

After 2030.

  • A 2031–35 fade:
    • AP EBITDA −2% a year in the base, as the CFM56 visit decline outweighs share gains;
    • Power flat;
    • Leasing & SCI +2%.
  • Cash conversion is held at the 2030 level, with fund co-investment normalised to growth needs.
  • Then a Gordon terminal value at 0% growth in the base.

12. Risks #

Risks to our short (upside for the stock):

  1. Power works:
    • an on-time first commissioning with good performance data;
    • a second customer independent of Jereh;
    • a unit count implying ≥$9M EBITDA per turbine. Our bull case ($328) is this world.
  2. DOE exempts behind-the-meter gensets, or J&F moves final assembly to the US.
  3. AP margin recovers above 32% as heavy-scope mix normalises and PMA parts ramp. Morgan Stanley’s bull case credits ~$0.8M of extra EBITDA per PMA module.
  4. SCI scales faster. The 2026 SPV’s $2.0B warehouse plus a $20B long-run AUM target lift fees and distributions.
  5. Technicals:
    • the $500M buyback executed near $167 (~3% of shares);
    • short interest 5.9% of float, 4.4 days to cover;
    • a Street that is almost all Buy and could defend the stock.
  6. A re-rating like other behind-the-meter suppliers. GE Vernova trades at ~27x and Bloom at ~48x 2027E EBITDA.

Risks that would deepen the downside:

  1. Q3/Q4 cash flow stays negative and inventory passes $1.7B.
  2. EO 14421 covers foreign-assembled behind-the-meter turbines, forcing re-sourcing or delay.
  3. CFM56 retirements jump with fuel above $4/gal, cutting future shop visits.
  4. MRE related-party share rises above 25% of AP revenue.
  5. Leverage: 2.65x net debt/EBITDA with a $1.0B 2028 maturity in a hiking rate cycle (Fed +25bp in Sep-26).
  6. Ownership concentration: Capital Group holds ~23%.

Backtest result. We tested whether fleet-activity data lead FTAI’s reported numbers: 135 tests, year-on-year Spearman at leads of 0–4 quarters, an out-of-sample comparison against persistence, and Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Alt data cannot yet nowcast FTAI:

  • the significant correlations reflect a shared 2022–24 recovery trend;
  • they fail out of sample.

FTAI’s growth is company-specific (share, capacity, related-party sales). The market series work as risk monitors (is the pond shrinking?), not revenue predictors. We state this plainly because it limits how much the alt data can say about any single quarter.
